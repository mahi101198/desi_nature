Ghee keeps well because there is very little in it that can spoil. It is made from milk, cream or butter by removing almost all of the water and the milk solids, which leaves a product that is nearly pure milk fat. Indian food regulations allow no more than 0.5 per cent moisture in ghee, and Agmark grading is stricter at 0.3 per cent.

That low moisture is why ghee does not need a fridge the way butter does. But "keeps well" is not the same as "keeps forever". Ghee still goes stale and then rancid, and an Indian kitchen, with its hot summers, humid monsoon and a stove that runs several times a day, gives it plenty of chances.

This guide covers what actually spoils ghee, how to store it through each season, which containers to use, and how to tell when a jar has turned.

## What spoils ghee

Training material on ghee packaging from NIFTEM, the national food technology institute, names four things that cause ghee to deteriorate: **light, air, water vapour and metals**. Each one leads to off flavours and, eventually, rancidity. Heat is the fifth factor, because it speeds up all of the others.

There are two ways ghee turns:

- **Oxidation.** Oxygen from the air reacts with the fat. Light and heat speed it up, and so do traces of metals such as copper and iron. Oxidised ghee smells stale or sharp, a little like old cooking oil or crayons.
- **Hydrolysis.** Water breaks fat down into free fatty acids, which taste sour and soapy. Ghee starts with almost no water, so this usually happens when water gets in later: a wet spoon, steam from a pot, or condensation inside the jar.

Almost every storage habit below is about keeping those five things away from the ghee.

## Storing ghee through the Indian year

### Summer

In much of India, kitchen temperatures in May and June can stay well above 30°C for weeks. Ghee will be fully liquid at those temperatures. That alone does no harm, but heat speeds up oxidation, so:

- Keep the jar in the coolest dark cupboard you have, away from the stove, oven, fridge exhaust and any wall that gets afternoon sun.
- Never keep ghee on the shelf right above the stove, however convenient. It gets heat and steam with every meal.
- If you have more ghee than you will use in a few weeks, move the extra into the fridge for the summer (see below).

### Monsoon

Humidity is the monsoon's risk. Moisture from the air is not the main problem; water carried in by people is.

- Use a completely dry spoon every time. A spoon that has just been rinsed, or one used to stir dal, brings both water and food particles.
- Close the lid as soon as you have taken what you need. An open jar next to a pressure cooker collects steam.
- Wipe the rim and the inside of the lid if water droplets form there.

If mould ever appears on the surface, water has got in. Throw that jar away rather than scraping the top off.

### Winter

In cooler months ghee sets solid or turns grainy. That is a normal change of texture with temperature, not spoilage. To soften it, stand the closed jar in a bowl of warm water for a few minutes, or scoop out what you need with a dry spoon. Avoid melting the whole jar again and again on the stove; repeated heating speeds up oxidation.

## Fridge or shelf?

Ghee does not need refrigeration for everyday use. A jar you finish within a few weeks is fine in a cool, dark cupboard.

The fridge is useful for **stock you will not open for a while**: a large tin bought in bulk, or ghee sent from home or from a farm. Cold slows oxidation. Two cautions:

1. Ghee in the fridge sets hard. Take out what you need with a dry spoon rather than warming the whole jar each time.
2. Taking a cold jar into a hot, humid kitchen and opening it straight away can draw condensation inside. Let it reach room temperature with the lid closed first, or take portions from it quickly and return it.

Ghee from the freezer works the same way, with the same condensation caution.

## The two-jar habit

The simplest change most households can make is to split ghee into two containers:

- **A small daily jar**, enough for a week or two, kept near where you cook (but not over the stove).
- **A larger stock jar**, sealed, kept in a dark cupboard or the fridge, and opened only to refill the small one.

The stock jar then meets air, light and spoons only a few times, instead of three times a day. If the small jar is ever contaminated, you lose a week's ghee, not the whole supply.

## Which container to use

| Container | Good for ghee? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glass jar with a tight lid | Yes | Does not react with fat. Clear glass lets in light, so keep it in a cupboard. |
| Food-grade stainless steel with a tight lid | Yes | Blocks light and does not react with ghee. |
| Ceramic or glazed earthenware with a lid | Yes, if the glaze is intact | Blocks light. Unglazed clay can absorb fat and smell. |
| Food-grade plastic tub | Short term | Fine for the daily jar; plastic can take on smells over time. |
| Copper, brass or unlined iron | No | These metals speed up oxidation of ghee. |

Whatever you choose, it should be completely dry before ghee goes in, have a lid that closes well, and be filled close to the top. A half-empty jar holds more air above the ghee. When you refill the daily jar, decant the stock into a smaller jar as it runs down.

## How to tell if ghee has gone bad

Use your nose first, then your eyes, then a small taste.

- **Smell.** Fresh ghee smells nutty and buttery. Rancid ghee smells sharp, sour, stale or like old paint, crayons or soap.
- **Look.** Mould spots, a pinkish or greenish tinge, or water separating at the bottom mean the jar should be thrown away. A colour change from pale to deeper yellow on its own is not a reliable sign either way.
- **Taste.** A tiny amount on the tongue: bitter, soapy or sour means it has turned.

Slightly stale ghee is not the same as unsafe ghee, but it will make food taste off. If you are not sure, or the ghee is for someone who is unwell, elderly or very young, do not use it.

Bought ghee carries a best-before date on its label. Treat it as the limit for an unopened pack stored as the label says, not as a promise once the jar has been opened and used in a hot kitchen.

## A quick checklist

- Dry spoon, every time.
- Lid back on straight away.
- Cool, dark cupboard, not above the stove.
- Glass, steel or glazed ceramic. No copper or brass.
- Small daily jar, larger sealed stock jar.
- Fridge for bulk stock in summer; let it warm with the lid closed before opening.
- Smell before you use an old jar.

## Buying ghee you can store well

Storage starts at the point of purchase. When you buy ghee, check that the label shows an FSSAI licence or registration number, a packing or manufacturing date and a best-before date, and that the seal is intact. For ghee from a farm or a small producer, ask when the batch was made and how it was packed.
